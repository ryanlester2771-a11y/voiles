# ReviewLead — Concept Pressure Test
## Five Filters Applied to the Top Five Concepts

**Compiled:** May 2026
**Purpose:** Stress-test the top five concepts against real conversion conditions before production launch. A concept that reads well in a document can fail the moment it hits a real person's feed. This document identifies exactly where each concept is vulnerable, what would need to change, and in what order they should go to production.
**Source documents:** `12_concepts_and_hooks.md` (concepts), `07_emotional_arc_narrative.md` (stage alignment), `08_constraint_analysis.md` (objection architecture), `09_competitor_constraint_analysis.md` (differentiation baseline), `10_constraint_gap_analysis.md` (gap reference)

---

## THE FIVE FILTERS

**Filter 1 — The Scroll Test:** Does the top hook stop the thumb at 10:47 PM, half-distracted, no context, not in buying mode? If it requires context, sounds like every other ad in this space, or doesn't create an immediate open loop — it fails.

**Filter 2 — The "That's Me" Test:** Is the concept aimed at the right stage of the emotional progression? A concept aimed at Stage 2 will repel a Stage 5 buyer and vice versa.

**Filter 3 — The Objection Test:** What constraints fire immediately when the prospect encounters this messaging — and does the concept have a built-in answer or does it get killed by them?

**Filter 4 — The Competitive Differentiation Test:** Could a competitor run this same concept with minor word changes? If yes, the concept is not anchored deeply enough in the specific gap ReviewLead owns.

**Filter 5 — The Funnel Coherence Test:** Does the emotional thread the concept starts continue naturally through to the sales page — or is there a jarring disconnect?

## CONCEPT 1 — THE WRONG PERSON IS WINNING

### Filter 1: The Scroll Test — PASSES

**Top hook:** *"Your competitor has 287 reviews. You have 31. You already know who does better work."*

The specific numbers create a pattern interrupt — not generic "you need more reviews" but two concrete quantities that force a calculation. The final sentence executes a moral flip: instead of creating anxiety ("you're losing"), it creates self-confirmation ("your judgment is correct"). That inversion is what stops the thumb — it's validating in a way that demands to know what comes next. No context is needed. A contractor at 10:47 PM sees those numbers and immediately recognizes: *this is about me, and someone knows what I believe about it.*

No fix needed on the primary hook.

---

### Filter 2: The "That's Me" Test — PASSES WITH NOTE

**Intercepts:** Stage 3 (Frustration and Failure), lean toward Stage 3-4

Stage 3 is where the competitor comparison becomes a recurring private ritual, where bitterness begins to edge in, and where the language shifts from "I need to be more consistent" to "I've been consistent and it's not working." The concept lives precisely here: the truck cab, the competitor search, the specific indignation of watching the wrong result declared publicly.

**Note:** The concept is most emotionally resonant for someone who has *watched* the competitor pull ahead over time — which is late Stage 3 into Stage 4, not pure Stage 3. Someone in early Stage 3 still believes effort will close the gap. The concept is most activating for a buyer who has already concluded effort is not the variable. This is not a fatal misalignment — it means the concept will over-index toward the most frustrated, most ready-to-act buyers in Stage 3, which is favorable.

---

### Filter 3: The Objection Test — CONDITIONAL

**Constraints that fire:**

**I-5 ("I earn things — I don't game systems"):** The moment he sees "here's the specific fix," his mind goes to: *is the fix legitimate? Am I being offered the thing I've already decided I won't do?*

**B-3 ("Any company promising to get me more reviews is probably doing something sketchy"):** The moral indignation frame works *until* he wonders if the correction involves manufactured reviews. Then the hook that was validating him becomes uncomfortable.

**V-1 (Quality over optics):** "Here's how to correct it" could sound like: *we'll inflate your review count to match the cheater.* That's optics management, not quality recognition.

**Does the concept handle these?** Partially, but not at the hook level. Hook 1C ("without compromising anything about how you operate") addresses it directly — but it's the third hook, not the lead. Hook 1A, the strongest scroll-stopper, leaves these constraints open. The click gets him to a landing page that *must* immediately answer: *"This isn't fake reviews — here's the specific, legitimate mechanism."* If the landing copy doesn't do this in the first 30 seconds, the objection kills the conversion.

**Vulnerability:** The concept earns the click but leaves the legitimacy constraint to be resolved by landing copy.

---

### Filter 4: The Competitive Differentiation Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

Technically, any competitor could write "your competitor has more reviews — you do better work — we fix that." But the moral frame only holds if the offer backs it up. The implicit promise of Hook 1A is: *this correction will honor your code. You won't have to game anything. You won't have to become a self-promoter.* Birdeye cannot back that promise — their platform requires becoming a software operator. Podium cannot back it — their process contradicts the craftsman identity. ReviewLead, with a fully managed model and zero owner involvement, is the only offer that actually delivers a correction "without compromising anything about how you operate."

The copy is surface-imitable. The backing story is not. The differentiation is structural to the offer, not just the words.

**Sharpening opportunity:** Hook 1E ("you've built 17 years of reputation — your competitor built 287 reviews — guess which one Google can read") is slightly more ownable because it makes the translation problem explicit rather than just the indignation.

---

### Filter 5: The Funnel Coherence Test — FLAG

This concept makes a specific emotional promise: *you're experiencing a moral injustice, and there's a legitimate correction.* A standard review management sales page — "get more Google reviews, rank higher, win more customers" — drops the moral frame on line one and converts the message into a generic growth pitch. The Stage 3 buyer who clicked because of righteous indignation arrives at feature bullets and testimonials and feels the bait-and-switch.

**What the page must do:**
- Open by acknowledging the injustice explicitly ("the algorithm is making the wrong call about who the best contractor in your market is — and that has a specific, fixable cause")
- Never use the word "marketing"
- Explain the mechanism in terms of legitimacy, not just efficacy
- Include a primary section: "here's how we correct this without asking you to become anything you're not"

**Biggest single vulnerability:** The hook earns the click; the legitimacy constraint must be immediately resolved on the landing page or the conversion dies.

## CONCEPT 2 — THE TRANSLATION PROBLEM

### Filter 1: The Scroll Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

**Top hook:** *"You built your reputation one job at a time. We make sure Google knows it."*

The hook is emotionally clean and identity-validating. "One job at a time" is precise. The problem: it's a *calm* hook in a medium that rewards disruption. At 10:47 PM, half-distracted, calm validation may not create enough of a pattern interrupt. The open loop is soft.

**Hook 2C is stronger for cold traffic:** *"This isn't marketing. Your reputation is already built. It just needs a translator."* The phrase "This isn't marketing" is a counter-claim that disrupts the category — someone who has been pitched marketing solutions for years hits that phrase and has to pause.

**Recommendation:** Hook 2C should be elevated to the primary creative test. Hook 2A belongs in secondary rotation for audiences who've already engaged with the brand.

---

### Filter 2: The "That's Me" Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

**Intercepts:** Stage 2-3 boundary (not pure Stage 2)

The concept is written for Stage 2, but Stage 2 buyers ("I just need to be more consistent") still believe effort will solve this. They're not yet open to "the algorithm doesn't read quality." The translation reframe lands most powerfully at the Stage 2-3 boundary — the person who has been trying to ask customers for 2-6 months, gotten inconsistent results, and is starting to feel confused about why effort isn't producing results.

**Risk at pure Stage 2:** The concept could convert to motivation to ask manually ("yes, I should probably do something about Google — I'll start asking customers more consistently"), not to hire ReviewLead.

**Implication:** This concept belongs in the Stage 2-3 transition audience, not earliest awareness. Running it cold to purely Stage 1-2 people risks educating them toward manual effort rather than toward the offer.

---

### Filter 3: The Objection Test — CONDITIONAL

**Constraints that fire:**

**I-1 ("I let my work speak for itself"):** "We make sure Google knows it" can be read as promotion, which is exactly what this person doesn't do.

**V-1 (Quality over optics):** "Making sure Google knows it" sounds like managing optics.

**B (Agencies over-promise and under-deliver):** The hook structure sounds like the beginning of a thousand ads this person has already seen.

**Does the concept handle these?** Hook 2C handles them better than 2A. "This isn't marketing. Your reputation is already built. It just needs a translator" — the phrase "this isn't marketing" is a direct objection kill. But it only works if the landing copy *immediately explains what it is instead*, with specificity.

**The conceptual risk:** The "translation" metaphor is powerful at the paragraph level but can feel like wordplay to a burned skeptic. *"That's a clever way of saying marketing. They all find clever ways of saying marketing."* What resolves this: immediate mechanism specificity. Not "translation" as metaphor — "translation" as mechanism: we reach your actual customers at the right moment after the job with a low-friction request.

---

### Filter 4: The Competitive Differentiation Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

The translation metaphor is genuinely unoccupied in competitor messaging. No one is making this argument. The underlying insight — "your reputation is real, it just needs to be made visible" — is adjacent to what Broadly gestures at, but the translation frame is more specific and more philosophically grounded.

**The vulnerability:** The underlying concept ("help your reputation show up online") is within reach of any competitor. NiceJob could write "your work is excellent — let your customers tell Google." Broadly could add "translation" language to their existing copy.

**What makes it ownable specifically for ReviewLead:** The translator "operates invisibly, between two languages, without changing the meaning of what was said." Only a fully managed, zero-owner-involvement service can claim to operate invisibly. Software products operate *through* the owner — they require him to be visible in the process. ReviewLead's model is the only one where the translator claim is mechanically true.

---

### Filter 5: The Funnel Coherence Test — FLAG (highest execution risk)

The promise: "This isn't marketing. Your reputation is already built. A translator makes it visible." This frame requires:
- The word "marketing" to never appear on the landing page
- All language to avoid: "boost your online presence," "grow your business," "reputation marketing"
- All copy to use: "make visible what's already there," "convert your reputation into a format Google reads," "your customers remember you — we capture that memory as a review"

**A single lapse into growth or marketing language breaks the promise entirely.** This is the highest-execution-risk concept in the top five. It cannot be maintained informally — it requires a copy rule document that every piece of customer-facing communication is checked against before publication.

**Biggest single vulnerability:** The "translation" frame breaks the moment any downstream copy uses standard marketing language. Execution consistency is the single greatest risk.

## CONCEPT 3 — THE BEGGING SHAME

### Filter 1: The Scroll Test — STRONG PASS

**Top hook:** *"Stop begging your customers for reviews. There's a system that does it without the awkward ask."*

"Begging" is the word they use in their own heads. When this word appears in an ad, it bypasses the normal filtering that marketing language triggers and lands directly on a private experience they've never seen named out loud. The scroll stops not because of clever construction but because of recognition: *someone knows what I've been doing and what I call it internally.*

"The awkward ask" sharpens it — "awkward" is precisely the texture of this experience. The open loop is strong: there's a system that removes this experience entirely — what is it?

This is the highest emotional recognition hook in the top five.

---

### Filter 2: The "That's Me" Test — PERFECT ALIGNMENT

**Intercepts:** Stage 3 (Frustration and Failure)

Stage 3 is when "begging" enters the internal monologue. This is when the customer who promised three times and never delivered sits like a specific wound. This is when the shame — a self-reliant man reduced to following up and being ignored — is active and unresolved.

The concept describes exactly this experience with exact emotional texture: the promise at the end of the job, the three follow-ups, the hug at the door, the no-review, the private shame of discovering that something he believed happened naturally requires a kind of hustle he never had to do.

No misalignment to flag.

---

### Filter 3: The Objection Test — CONDITIONAL

**Constraints that fire:**

**I-4 ("I've been burned and I'm not naive"):** "There's a system" triggers: *what kind of system? An app I have to set up? Software with a monthly fee that produces almost nothing?*

**V-2 (Authenticity):** "Does it without the awkward ask" could mean it pressures customers through automated texts. That's manipulative, which violates his values around honest customer relationships.

**B-3 (Any company promising this is probably sketchy):** "A system that removes the ask entirely" — is this a bot? Incentivized reviews? The kind of thing that gets a Google profile flagged?

**Does the concept handle these?**

Hook 3A: **Does not.** "There's a system that does it without the awkward ask" leaves every objection open.

Hook 3C: **Does better.** Frames the problem as mechanism failure, which naturally leads into the legitimacy explanation.

Hook 3E: **Does better.** Social proof framing validates the shared experience while positioning ReviewLead as the category with the answer.

**The landing page must immediately resolve:** *"Here is specifically what happens. A member of our team sends a text to your customer between 24 and 48 hours after job completion. It goes from a real number. It is one sentence with a direct link. Nothing automated. Nothing bot-generated. No pressure. The customer is happy to do it — they just needed the timing to be right and the friction to be zero."* That paragraph must appear within 15 seconds of the click.

---

### Filter 4: The Competitive Differentiation Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

This is the most surface-imitable concept of the five. NiceJob, Signpost, and any automated review tool can claim to "remove the awkward ask" because their software automates the text request. A software automation IS less awkward than an in-person verbal ask — so the claim is technically defensible by competitors.

**What makes it ownable for ReviewLead:** ReviewLead removes the ask from the owner's *experience entirely.* The owner never initiates, monitors, follows up, or knows it happened until a new review appears. Software automation still requires the owner to: set up the sequence, connect the CRM, monitor the dashboard, see notifications, make decisions. ReviewLead's claim — "you never ask" — is categorically more accurate than "we automate the ask."

**Required fix before launch:** The hook should include the model distinction: *"Stop begging your customers for reviews. There's a service that does it for you — no app, no dashboard, no setup. You hand over your customer list. That's your entire job."* This addition makes the differentiation unambiguous and closes the "is this just another app?" objection before it can form.

---

### Filter 5: The Funnel Coherence Test — STRONG PASS (best natural coherence)

The emotional promise: "You will never have to ask for a review again. The asking happens automatically, correctly timed, without you."

ReviewLead's offer mechanics — hand over customer list, team handles everything, owner never logs in — directly fulfill this promise. The landing page doesn't require unusual architecture to maintain coherence. It just has to explain the mechanism clearly: here is what happens after you give us your customer list. Here is who contacts your customer. Here is when. Here is what they say. Here is what you never have to do.

This is the most structurally coherent concept in the library. The ad promise and the offer mechanics are naturally aligned.

**Biggest single vulnerability:** The top hook leaves the "is this sketchy?" objection open. Landing copy must immediately resolve this with mechanism specificity — not just "we get real reviews" (every competitor says this), but the specific process step by step.

## CONCEPT 4 — THE WRONG EXPERIMENT

### Filter 1: The Scroll Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

**Top hook:** *"Why your customers promised to leave a review — and didn't. (It's not what you think.)"*

The hook hits a real, universal experience and creates an open loop. The problem: "(It's not what you think.)" is one of the most common curiosity-gap constructions in digital advertising. A Stage 3-4 buyer who has been actively searching for answers may recognize the pattern and scroll past — not because the topic doesn't resonate but because the format feels familiar.

**Hook 4D is stronger for cold traffic:** *"The reason you got 3 reviews from 50 happy customers isn't your customers. It's the mechanism."* This is more specific and more directly exonerating — it kills the "my customers aren't the type" belief while simultaneously creating curiosity about "the mechanism."

**Recommendation:** 4D should be elevated to the primary test for cold traffic. 4A is better for re-targeting audiences who've already engaged with related content.

---

### Filter 2: The "That's Me" Test — CONDITIONAL PASS WITH CONDESCENSION RISK

**Intercepts:** Stage 3 (well), with Stage 4 risk

At Stage 3, the "my customers don't leave reviews" conclusion is live and unsettled — frustrating but not yet fully calcified. The "wrong experiment" reframe arrives while the conclusion is still movable, which is the right timing.

**The Stage 4 risk:** A Stage 4 buyer has been carrying this conclusion for months. It's now protective — easier to believe "my customers aren't the type" than to believe the method was wrong. Being told "you ran the wrong experiment" at Stage 4 can register as: *you're saying I've been wrong about this for two years and I didn't figure it out?* That's an identity threat, not an insight.

**Hook 4D handles this more safely:** "It's not your customers. It's the mechanism." This says explicitly: your judgment about your customers was correct — while introducing the mechanism as the actual variable. This is more identity-compatible than "you ran the wrong experiment," which locates the failure at the owner's level.

---

### Filter 3: The Objection Test — WEAKEST OF THE FIVE

**Constraints that fire:**

**I-4 ("I've been burned"):** "Here's why it didn't work before" is precisely the pitch every agency gave him before underdelivering. Every vendor has a story about why their approach is different.

**B ("I tried before. It probably won't work for me."):** The mechanism education can produce the wrong conversion. He reads the 24-hour window data, thinks "that makes sense," and concludes: *I'll try texting them faster next time.* The concept educates him to try a slightly better version of what he's already tried — not to hire ReviewLead.

**I-1 ("I let my work speak for itself"):** Being told "you ran the experiment wrong" locates the failure in his behavior, threatening the identity of someone who believes he's been doing everything right.

**The critical missing element:** The concept lacks a uniqueness anchor that makes the conclusion point unambiguously to ReviewLead. The educational content converts people into better manual attempts, not into ReviewLead customers.

**The argument that needs to be added:** *"The right experiment requires: your customer reached within 24-48 hours of job completion, reliably, regardless of what else happened that day. A text from someone who isn't you, so there's no relationship awkwardness. A link with zero friction. Three requirements. None of them can be met by you personally — not because of effort or consistency, but because your schedule is structurally incompatible with the timing requirement. The right experiment is only run by someone whose entire job is running it."*

Without that paragraph, concept 4 is an awareness asset that benefits the category. With it, it becomes a ReviewLead-specific conversion tool.

---

### Filter 4: The Competitive Differentiation Test — FAILS IN CURRENT FORM

This is the most imitable concept of the five. The mechanism data (24-hour window, 70% rule, friction effect) is industry knowledge. BrightLocal publishes it. NiceJob has blog posts about review timing. Any competitor can publish this education — and some already have.

A contractor reading this concept could equally conclude: "I should use NiceJob's automation" or "I should set up a Podium sequence" or "I should try harder with manual timing." The concept inadvertently educates people into competitor solutions.

**What would make it ownable:** The uniqueness anchor described above — the argument that the mechanism requires an external operator, not just a better tool or better personal timing. This converts the general insight into a ReviewLead-specific conclusion.

**Do not kill this concept — rebuild it.** The underlying insight is powerful. The fix is surgical.

---

### Filter 5: The Funnel Coherence Test — CONDITIONAL PASS

The hook promises: "I will explain why it didn't work and show you what the right approach looks like." A standard sales page breaks this promise. A page that leads with education before features fulfills it.

**Required page architecture:**
1. Validate the experience (you tried, it didn't work, here's why that conclusion makes sense)
2. Deliver the education (24-hour window, friction effect, 70% rule — specifically)
3. Explain why ReviewLead's model is the only correct execution of this mechanism
4. Social proof from people who previously tried and failed manually, then succeeded with ReviewLead
5. CTA

This structure is achievable without rebuilding the whole page — it requires adding a "why manual asking fails" section before the offer section.

**Biggest single vulnerability:** Without the uniqueness anchor, this concept educates prospects into competitors. With it, it becomes one of the highest-trust conversion concepts in the library.

## CONCEPT 5 — THE BURNED SKEPTIC'S MATH

### Filter 1: The Scroll Test — CONDITIONAL PASS (Stage 5-6 only)

**Top hook:** *"When you stop paying for ads, the leads stop. Google reviews don't work that way."*

For someone in Stage 5-6 who has been burned by ad spend, the first sentence triggers the specific memory of the agency failure immediately. For that person, this hook stops the scroll.

For cold traffic — someone at Stage 2 or Stage 3 who hasn't yet gone down the agency path — the hook is interesting but not visceral. It requires the burned experience to fully land.

**Hook 5D is stronger for Stage 5 cold traffic:** *"Before you put this in the same category as the last agency that burned you — read the one thing that makes it structurally different."* This names the experience, validates the categorization reflex, and creates a precise open loop.

**This concept should not run as cold traffic to broad audiences.** It belongs in Stage 5-6 targeting, re-targeting, and lookalike audiences built from engaged Stage 3-4 traffic.

---

### Filter 2: The "That's Me" Test — PERFECT ALIGNMENT

**Intercepts:** Stage 5-6

Stage 5 language: "I've been burned. Whatever I try next will probably disappoint me again." Stage 6 language: "Doing nothing is also a choice. And it's the wrong one." The concept describes the exact specific experience — the agency, the seven months, the $5,600, the spreadsheet without revenue, the feeling of having been made a fool — and then offers not enthusiasm but a structural reason this is different.

This is exactly what Stage 5-6 needs. Not "try again." A proof that this is a categorically different bet.

---

### Filter 3: The Objection Test — STRONGEST OF THE FIVE

This concept is essentially built *from* the objection. Its structure is: here is the objection (you've been burned, you have a rule, the rule is reasonable) — here is the specific, structural reason this is different — here is why the rule doesn't apply.

**Constraints that fire and how the concept handles them:**

- **I-4 ("I've been burned"):** Addressed directly in the concept paragraph and hook 5D — the concept acknowledges the burning before the pitch
- **B (Agencies over-promise):** Addressed by the permanent asset / rented traffic structural distinction
- **B ("Affordable = scam"):** The permanent asset argument reframes the value proposition so $249/month is evaluated as asset-building investment, not agency retainer
- **R (Trust as depleted resource):** The structural distinction is delivered before any commitment is requested

**One specific vulnerability:** The permanent asset argument requires him to believe reviews are actually permanent. He may have heard about reviews being filtered or removed. If he doesn't believe "if I generate 100 reviews, they stay on my profile," the entire structural differentiation fails.

**Required verification copy:** *"Google removed 188 million fake reviews in 2024 — not legitimate reviews from real customers. Reviews from people who actually used your service are not removed. Here's specifically what that distinction means for what we build."*

---

### Filter 4: The Competitive Differentiation Test — STRONGEST OF THE FIVE

"When you stop paying for ads, the leads stop. Google reviews don't work that way." — Any competitor who also sells ads management, SEO, or social media advertising cannot run this hook without being hypocritical. The hook positions ad spend as the alternative being dismissed. Birdeye, Podium, and Broadly all sell ads products. Running this hook would undermine their other product lines.

ReviewLead's single-product model — nothing but review management — is the only context in which this claim is completely credible and internally consistent. You can only genuinely say "ads traffic disappears when you stop paying, reviews don't" if you're not also selling ads.

This is the only concept in the top five where the differentiation is structural to the *business model*, not just the copy. It can be copied as a sentence. It cannot be credibly backed by any multi-product competitor.

---

### Filter 5: The Funnel Coherence Test — FLAG (most radical page restructure required)

The emotional promise: *"You've been burned. This is structurally different. Here's the specific math on why."*

A standard review management sales page breaks this promise in approximately 3 seconds. The moment the page opens with "Get more Google reviews in 7 days" or "Boost your online reputation," the burned skeptic is gone.

**What the page must do instead:**

1. **Open with the burning experience named explicitly.** Not a footnote — a direct acknowledgment: *"If you've paid a marketing agency before and got reports without results, everything we say here is going to sound exactly like what they said. We know that. Here's specifically why it isn't."*

2. **Deliver the structural differentiation immediately.** The permanent asset argument is the second element on the page, not buried in a FAQ.

3. **Show the math.** Not just "reviews are permanent" — calculate what 50 reviews means in ranking position, what that means in monthly click share, what click share means in calls, what calls mean in revenue. Make the asset case with arithmetic, not assertion.

4. **Address the trust deficit with a risk structure.** A performance guarantee or first-review proof mechanism must be present. The burned skeptic does not extend goodwill. The page must offer something before asking for full commitment.

This concept requires the most investment in sales page architecture of the five — but it also has the highest potential conversion rate for the highest-intent buyer.

**Biggest single vulnerability:** The "permanent asset" claim can be challenged if he knows reviews can occasionally be filtered. Verification copy addressing this must be present before the argument can stand.

## PRODUCTION RECOMMENDATION

### Go to Production First: Concepts 3 and 1 (in that order)

**Concept 3 (The Begging Shame) — Launch first:**
- Highest emotional recognition at the hook level ("begging" is verbatim language from the research corpus)
- Perfect Stage 3 alignment — the largest, most addressable audience segment
- Best natural funnel coherence — offer mechanics fulfill the hook promise without unusual page architecture
- **One fix before launch:** Sharpen the top hook to include model specificity: *"Stop begging your customers for reviews. There's a service that does it for you — no app, no dashboard, no setup. You hand over your customer list. That's your entire job."* This closes the "is this just another app?" objection before it forms.

**Concept 1 (The Wrong Person Is Winning) — Launch second:**
- Strongest scroll-stop in the library
- Moral indignation frame is completely unoccupied by any competitor
- Model uniquely backs the promise
- **Required fix:** Landing page must open with the moral frame and immediately address legitimacy — this is a page architecture task, not a copy overhaul

**Concept 5 (The Burned Skeptic's Math) — Launch third as re-targeting, not cold traffic:**
- Perfect Stage 5-6 alignment makes it the right second or third touchpoint for people who've engaged with Concepts 3 or 1 but haven't converted
- As cold traffic it's too audience-specific (only works for burned buyers who identify with the agency failure narrative)
- Strongest structural differentiation in the library — run it when you know you're talking to someone who's been down this road
- **Before launch:** Add verification copy addressing the "reviews can be filtered" objection; add a concrete risk-reversal offer in the first 30 days

### Do Not Kill Concept 4 — Rebuild It

In current form, the educational content benefits the category rather than ReviewLead specifically. The fix is surgical: add the uniqueness anchor.

**Add this argument to the concept and the hook rotation:**

*"The right experiment requires your customer reached within 24-48 hours of job completion, reliably, regardless of what else happened that day. A message from someone who isn't you, so there's no relationship awkwardness. A link with zero friction. These three requirements together mean the right experiment cannot be run by you personally — not because of your effort or consistency, but because your schedule is structurally incompatible with the timing requirement. The only experiment that consistently produces the 70% conversion is one run by someone whose entire job is running it."*

That paragraph converts the education into a ReviewLead-specific conclusion. Without it, Concept 4 is a powerful awareness piece that inadvertently benefits competitors. With it, it becomes a high-trust conversion concept that belongs in the mid-funnel.

### Hold Concept 2 Until Execution Infrastructure Is Ready

The Translation Problem is the highest-upside brand positioning concept in the library — and the highest-risk when executed inconsistently. The "translation" frame is too fragile to maintain informally. It requires a copy rule document governing every piece of customer-facing communication before launch:

- The word "marketing" is banned from all copy
- "Boost your reputation," "grow your business," "online presence" are banned
- All copy uses: "make visible what's already earned," "translate your reputation," "a format Google can read"
- Every ad, email, sales page section, and sales conversation is checked against this guide before publication

When that infrastructure is in place, Concept 2 becomes the most powerful long-term brand positioning in the market. Launched without it, a single lapse breaks the frame and costs more than not running it.
